Playgrounds are a growing child's world, it's what they explore in & what they problem solve for. To represent such a child-like concept then, requires a whimsically equivalent world as a front, with no shortage of (kinetic) movement. Well, all thanks to the bears that ride bikes, I had the opportunity to bring such an experience into the world, wide web.

This project had no shortage of difficult problems, with the main one being the custom 3D pipeline required for a client-customisable spin-around of their playground models. Whilst this was a really annoying problem to solve, it also opened the doors to push the visual limits of Three.js, & explore what the browser is truly capable of when given the requirement of video generation.

Wait, you said 3D renderer?

The majority of the R&D for this project was spent on the aforementioned 3D renderer for the products. This was a admin-facing, client-side 3D model renderer, that produced & uploaded a final video, to be associated with a product. It was implemented as a mini React app in the Wordpress dashboard, without a build tool.

The next step then, was the rendering. This was a different beast; let me show you the rendering progresses & statuses, before explaining what happens during the rendering.

SSGI Sampling

"Current Sample" shows us the pre-frame progress where the renderer performs Screen Space Global Illumination (SSGI) for 30 passes, which gives us realistic diffusions & lighting based on the environment & the model itself. The implementation for this uses a fork of 0beqz/realism-effects.

Whilst SSGI is not strictly required for this project, I took the liberty to push for it, as it provides visual fidelity only possible due to "offline" rendering (aka. non-real-time). It was a good opportunity, as this was probably the one niche use-case of browser 3D that let me push per-frame render times into the seconds. Hell, even single-sample SSGI remains too demanding for office-level computers & mobile, & that disregards how on a single page there could be 10+ products displayed at once, each with model files being MBs large.

In-browser capture + encoding

In order to turn what was rendered into a usable, uploadable video, I used my previous expertise on finangling the in-browser ffmpeg.wasm library. It's not a beast I will pretend to fully comprehend, & I don't think it will be a beast I will ever fully be able to utilise (it's been sort of unmaintained ever since it was released). Though, the love for it I have will prevail, since it lets you do weird things like this renderer, & Auto Intro.

"Current Transport Frame" show us for the frame, how the canvas is asynchronously captured into a png image & "transported" over to the encoder worker's memory.

"Current Encoding Frame" stays 0 for most of the actual rendering process, only beginning to increment after all the frames are captured & moved. At the end, FFmpeg consumes the transported frames & encodes the video into a h264 file.

Uploading

Finally, the video is uploaded to another custom API & fitted to the product's post. Yippie.

Reflection

Was this project a fun challenge? Absolutely. However, it wasn't smooth sailing & I have sea sickness. The design was out of my domain, & had to be mutated many of times, to all end-of-production-line developers' dismay. Though at the end of the day, I can't complain too hard. I got to ultimately exercise my extremely niche knowledge & develop on lots of weird techniques for all the sticky-ness happening. I hope all the playground shoppers in the future enjoy the whimsicality they stumble upon, & appreciate how the corner radii of the footer changes to mesh with the floating image.
